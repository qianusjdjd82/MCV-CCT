Chapter Overview

This chapter builds on Chapter 8 (equations for lines and planes in 3-space). We analyse geometric relationships: intersection, parallel and coincident cases for lines and planes. We also solve linear systems and calculate perpendicular shortest distance between points, lines and planes.

Learning Goals

  • Identify all possible intersection cases for lines and planes in \(R^3\)
  • Solve systems of linear equations and link algebraic results to 3D-geometry
  • Apply distance formulas for point-to-line and point-to-plane
Big Idea of Chapter 9: Connect 3-D space geometry with linear algebra. The number of solutions (0, one, infinitely many) of a linear system tells us how these lines and planes intersect. Finally we compute perpendicular shortest distances.

9.1 The Intersection of a Line with a Plane and the Intersection of Two Lines

Definition

  • Skew lines: Two lines in \(R^3\) that are not parallel and do not intersect. Skew lines only exist in 3-dimensional space.
  • Intersecting lines: Two lines share exactly one common point.
  • Coincident lines: Two lines lie exactly on top of each other; infinitely many shared points.
  • Parallel lines: Direction vectors are scalar multiples of each other.

Core Concepts

Possible cases for two lines in \(R^3\):
1. Intersect at exactly one point;  2. Coincident;  3. Parallel distinct;  4. Skew (only for \(R^3\)).

Possible cases for a line and a plane:

  1. Intersect at one single point
  2. Line is parallel to the plane with no intersection
  3. The entire line lies inside the plane (infinitely many intersection points)

Solving strategy: Substitute the parametric equations of the line into the Cartesian plane equation, solve for parameter \(t\), judge cases from the number of valid \(t\)-solutions.

Important Formulas

Vector equation for line: \(\vec{r} = \vec{r}_0 + t\vec{d}\)

Parametric line: \(x = x_0 + td_x,\ y = y_0 + td_y,\ z = z_0 + td_z\)

Cartesian plane: \(Ax + By + Cz + D = 0\)

Quick Example

Given a line’s parametric equations, substitute \(x, y, z\) into plane’s Cartesian equation. If you get one value for \(t\) \(\rightarrow\) one intersection point. If \(t\) cancels and you get a true statement (\(0=0\)), the line lies on the plane.

9.2 Systems of Linear Equations

Definition

A system of linear equations consists of two or more linear equations involving the same variables. In three-dimensional space (\(R^3\)), a linear system typically consists of three equations with three variables (\(x, y, z\)), each representing a plane.

Core Concepts

Possible solution sets for a 3-variable linear system:

  1. Unique solution: The planes intersect at a single point \((x, y, z)\).
  2. Infinitely many solutions: The planes intersect along a common line or are coincident.
  3. No solution (Inconsistent): The system has contradictions (e.g., parallel planes or a triangular prism configuration with no common intersection).

Solving Strategy: Use Gaussian elimination (elementary row operations) on the augmented matrix \([A | B]\) to transform the system into row-echelon form.

Important Formulas

Augmented Matrix Form: \([A | B]\) where \(A\) is the coefficient matrix and \(B\) is the constant column vector.

Elementary Row Operations:
1. Interchange two rows (\(R_i \leftrightarrow R_j\)).
2. Multiply a row by a non-zero scalar (\(kR_i \to R_i\)).
3. Add a multiple of one row to another (\(R_i + kR_j \to R_i\)).

Quick Example

For a system like \(x+y+z=6\), \(2x-y+3z=14\), and \(x+2y-z=2\), applying row operations leads to upper triangular form, allowing back-substitution to find the unique solution \((1, 2, 3)\).

9.3 The Intersection of Two Planes

Definition

Two planes in \(R^3\) can either be parallel distinct, coincident, or intersecting along a straight line.

Core Concepts

Cases:

  1. Parallel distinct: Normal vectors are scalar multiples (\(\vec{n}_1 = k\vec{n}_2\)), but constant terms do not match ratio; no intersection.
  2. Coincident: Normal vectors and constants are proportional; infinite solutions (the whole plane).
  3. Intersecting: Normal vectors are not scalar multiples; they intersect along a line. The direction vector of the line is given by \(\vec{d} = \vec{n}_1 \times \vec{n}_2\).

Important Formulas

Line of intersection direction vector: \(\vec{d} = \vec{n}_1 \times \vec{n}_2\)

Quick Example

Cross the normal vectors of two non-parallel planes to get the direction vector of their intersection line, then find a particular point by setting one variable to zero.

9.1 Practice Questions

Q1: Determine whether the lines \(\vec{r} = (1, 2, 3) + t(1, -1, 2)\) and \(\vec{r} = (2, 1, 5) + s(2, -2, 4)\) are parallel, intersecting, coincident, or skew.

Solution: Direction vectors are \(\vec{d}_1 = (1, -1, 2)\) and \(\vec{d}_2 = (2, -2, 4) = 2\vec{d}_1\), so they are parallel. Testing point \((2, 1, 5)\) in the first line yields a valid consistent parameter \(t=1\). Thus, the lines are coincident.

Q2: Find the intersection point of the line \(x = 1+t, y = 2-t, z = 3+2t\) and the plane \(2x + y - z + 4 = 0\).

Solution: Substitute parametric equations into the plane: \(2(1+t) + (2-t) - (3+2t) + 4 = 0 \implies 5 - t = 0 \implies t = 5\). Substituting \(t=5\) gives the point \((6, -3, 13)\).

Q3: Explain why skew lines can only exist in three-dimensional space (\(R^3\) or higher) and not in two-dimensional space (\(R^2\)).

Solution: In \(R^2\), all lines share the same flat plane, meaning non-parallel lines must cross and parallel lines share the same plane. In \(R^3\), lines can exist at different elevations without intersecting and without being parallel (skew lines).

Q4: Determine if the line \(\vec{r} = (0, 1, 2) + t(1, 1, 1)\) intersects the plane \(x - y + z = 5\).

Solution: Substitute \(x = t, y = 1+t, z = 2+t\) into the plane equation: \(t - (1+t) + (2+t) = 5 \implies 1 + t = 5 \implies t = 4\). The intersection point is \((4, 5, 6)\).

Q5: Given a line with direction vector \(\vec{d} = (2, 3, -1)\) and a plane with normal vector \(\vec{n} = (4, -1, 5)\), what is their geometric relationship?

Solution: Compute dot product \(\vec{d} \cdot \vec{n} = 2(4) + 3(-1) + (-1)(5) = 8 - 3 - 5 = 0\). Since \(\vec{d} \perp \vec{n}\), the line is **parallel to the plane** (or lies within it).

9.2 Practice Questions

Q1: State the three possible types of solution sets for a linear system of three equations in three variables.

Solution: 1. Unique solution (single point); 2. Infinitely many solutions (line or plane); 3. No solution (inconsistent system).

Q2: Solve the linear system: \(x + y + z = 6\), \(2x - y + 3z = 14\), \(x + 2y - z = 2\).

Solution: Using Gaussian elimination, the unique solution is \(x = 1, y = 2, z = 3\).

Q3: What does a resulting statement like \(0 = 5\) signify during the row reduction of a linear system?

Solution: It means the system is **inconsistent** and has no solution.

Q4: Solve the dependent system: \(x + y = 3\) and \(2x + 2y = 6\).

Solution: The second equation is a multiple of the first. There are **infinitely many solutions**: \(x = t, y = 3 - t\) (\(t \in R\)).

Q5: What are the three elementary row operations used in Gaussian elimination?

Solution: 1. Interchange two rows; 2. Multiply a row by a non-zero scalar; 3. Add a multiple of one row to another row.

9.3 Practice Questions

Q1: What are the possible geometric relationships between two distinct planes in \(R^3\)?

Solution: They can either be **parallel distinct** (no intersection) or **intersecting along a straight line**.

Q2: Determine the intersection of the planes \(x + y + z = 3\) and \(2x + 2y + 2z = 8\).

Solution: The second plane simplifies to \(x + y + z = 4\). Since constants differ while normal vectors match, they are **parallel distinct (no solution)**.

Q3: How do you find the direction vector of the line of intersection of two intersecting planes?

Solution: By taking the **cross product** of their normal vectors: \(\vec{d} = \vec{n}_1 \times \vec{n}_2\).

Q4: Find the direction vector for the line of intersection between \(x + y - z = 1\) and \(2x - y + z = 2\).

Solution: \(\vec{d} = (1, 1, -1) \times (2, -1, 1) = (0, -3, -3)\) or proportional to \((0, 1, 1)\).

Q5: If two planes have normal vectors that are scalar multiples (\(\vec{n}_1 = k\vec{n}_2\)), what can be said about their intersection?

Solution: They are either parallel distinct (no solution) or coincident (infinitely many solutions).
